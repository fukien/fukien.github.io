Keeping a research notebook: a practical introduction

A practical introduction to keeping a research notebook, with a focus on clear assumptions, useful measurements, and practical tradeoffs.

On this page
  1. The starting question
  2. A small experiment
  3. What to look for

The starting question

This generated example explores keeping a research notebook. Imagine a workload that performs well on a small input but becomes less predictable as the data grows. The first task is to describe exactly what changed: the input distribution, the amount of parallel work, or the machine configuration.

A useful experiment begins with a question that can be tested. Rather than changing several parameters together, keep the workload fixed and vary one factor at a time. Record both the expected behavior and the observations that would challenge that expectation.

A small experiment

For this illustrative research notes exercise, compare a simple baseline with one targeted change. Use the same input, repeat the measurement, and report the variation between runs. The purpose is to make the comparison understandable rather than to produce a particular result.

Notebook example 28: start with a single worker, then increase parallelism gradually. Capture elapsed time and resource utilization alongside throughput. If performance stops improving, investigate where workers spend their time before proposing another optimization.

What to look for

Look for an explanation that accounts for more than the fastest run. A useful conclusion should also explain the slow cases and identify the conditions under which the approach becomes less effective. Separate measured evidence from possible explanations.

The next step for keeping a research notebook would be to repeat the experiment with a different input distribution and a larger working set. These sample notes intentionally contain no claimed experimental results; they exist to exercise the reading layout, archive filters, and navigation.
